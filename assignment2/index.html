1. Problem Statement

Monocular depth estimation means predicting, for every pixel of a single RGB image, how far that point is from the camera. It is useful for robots, AR and 3D scene understanding when only one normal camera is available. The hard part is that one image does not contain real scale: a small room close to the camera and a big hall far away can look the same. The model must learn scale from cues like object size, perspective and texture.

Our dataset mixes indoor and outdoor scenes, so the depth range is very different: most indoor pixels are 1–9 m, while outdoor goes up to 100–200 m. Ground truth comes from a laser scanner, so some pixels have no valid depth (sky, glass, object edges). These pixels must be ignored in training and evaluation.

ItemDefinition
InputOne RGB image, 3 × 288 × 384 (H × W)
OutputDepth map, 1 × 288 × 384, in metres
SupervisionGround-truth depth d* and validity mask m (1 = valid). Loss and metrics use only pixels with m = 1
FormulationLearn fθ: image → depth, minimising a masked loss between fθ(x) and d* over valid pixels

2.3 Data-splitting plan and leakage unit

The split unit is the scene. One scene has many scans and many views of the same place, so splitting by image would put almost the same picture in train and test. We assign whole scenes to one split (seed 42), about 70/15/15 by samples (69.4% / 15.3% / 15.3%). Each split has both indoor and outdoor scenes.

2.4 Subset selection rules

  • Random sampling inside each scene with a cap: at most 700 samples per indoor scene and 400 per outdoor scene, seed 42.
  • Why the cap: without it, outdoor samples came from only 2 of the 12 outdoor scenes. The cap forces the subset to cover more scenes instead of taking many images of one place.
  • No filtering by camera angle, depth or image content.
  • All images resized from 1024×768 to 384×288 to make the data small enough to store and train on a laptop (~2.5 GB).
  • Source path of every sample is kept in subset_metadata.csv, so the subset can be rebuilt.

3. Preliminary Distribution Analysis

Script: scripts/eda.py, run on all 7,195 samples. Depth statistics use only valid pixels (mask = 1) and the raw data (no depth cap).

3.1 Visual check

  • Depth matches the image: lamps and pipes are closer than the ceiling; on the outdoor path depth grows from the bottom to the buildings (~40–50 m). No shift and no flipped near/far.
  • Indoor invalid pixels are thin lines on object edges, where depth jumps (typical for laser scanners).
  • Outdoor invalid areas are sky, thin branches and far objects. The crane image is mostly sky, only 6.9% valid.
  • Very large values (e.g. 47.9 m in a 5 m room) are a few single pixels, not a broken file.

  • Far pixels appear in many images, but only a few per image.
  • Not all far indoor depth is noise: scan 00006_00058 is an indoor atrium where almost every pixel is 35–40 m. A cap at 20 or 30 m would delete real data.
  • Decision: pixels deeper than 50 m (indoor) or 200 m (outdoor) are marked invalid. Each cap removes less than 0.01% of valid pixels. We do not clamp them to the cap, because that would keep a wrong label. The caps are chosen on train only.

3.4 Scene and camera-angle distribution

The vertical angle code goes from 000 (level) to 050 (looking up at ceiling or sky). The horizontal code covers 000–350, and all 15 scenes have all 36 horizontal and all 6 vertical codes.

  • About 1/3 of the images look up (code ≥ 040): train 29.3% indoor / 31.9% outdoor, val 34.7% / 31.5%, test 35.4% / 25.8%. No strong bias between splits.
  • Only small differences between scenes: scene 00004 rarely looks up (9%), scenes 00003 / 00005 / 00008 about 40%.
  • No view looks down at the floor. We checked all 26,229 images of the full dataset on Kaggle: DIODE itself only has vertical codes 000–050, so this is not caused by our sampling.

Computed on valid pixels only, at 384×288, in metres. d = prediction, d* = ground truth, N = number of valid pixels.

  • Reported for all test images and separately for indoor and outdoor, because their depth ranges differ by about 10× and RMSE would otherwise be dominated by outdoor.
  • Abs Rel on val is used to pick the best checkpoint.
  • Compute cost is also reported: parameters, training time, inference time per image, peak VRAM.

6. Planned Methods

6.1 Baseline

A small U-Net (encoder–decoder with skip connections) trained from scratch. It predicts log-depth and is trained with a masked scale-invariant log loss (SILog, Eigen et al., 2014). Same data, split, preprocessing and number of epochs as the pretrained model. It shows how far we get without pretraining.

6.2 Pretrained model

  • MiDaS small (intel-isl/MiDaS, EfficientNet-Lite3 encoder, 21.3 M params), pretrained on a mix of depth datasets.
  • Zero-shot reference: MiDaS outputs relative inverse depth, not metres. For this row only, we align each prediction to the ground truth with a least-squares scale and shift (on valid pixels, in inverse-depth space), then convert to depth. This uses ground truth at test time, so it is only a reference.
  • Fine-tuning: the MiDaS output is trained as inverse depth (depth = 1 / output) with the same masked SILog loss, so the model predicts metres directly. AdamW, batch 8, 20 epochs, lower learning rate for the encoder than the decoder, checkpoint with the best val Abs Rel.
  • Backup if MiDaS small does not work well: DPT or Depth Anything (small).

8. Known Limitations

  1. Val and test each have only one indoor and one outdoor scene, so their numbers describe one place, not indoor/outdoor in general.
  2. Val/test indoor rooms are smaller than train (median 1.3–1.8 m vs 2.6 m), and train has a large atrium that val/test do not have. Indoor errors on val/test may look better than on bigger rooms.
  3. Val outdoor has ~47% invalid pixels (train ~31%), so fewer pixels are scored there.
  4. Labels on object edges and on bright or reflective surfaces are noisy. The mask removes most of it, but not all.
  5. DIODE has no downward views (vertical angle 000–050 only), so the model may not work well for a camera looking at the floor (e.g. a robot or drone).
  6. Images are 384×288, so thin objects and far details are harder to predict than at full resolution.
